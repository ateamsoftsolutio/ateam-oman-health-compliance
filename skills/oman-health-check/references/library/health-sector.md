## How to read this file

- **ID**: a stable identifier for one requirement (for example `PDPL-07`). IDs are not legal references; the legal reference is in the *Source* field.
- **Requirement**: what the rule requires, in plain language.
- **Engineering implication**: what the build team has to design, configure or produce.
- **Source**: the instrument, plus the article or clause number **only where it was checked** against the text, with a URL.
- **Confidence**:
  - **Confirmed (primary)**: checked against the official text, or its reproduction on qanoon.om with the Official Gazette reference, or a government website or form.
  - **Confirmed (secondary)**: taken from a reputable law-firm guide, academic paper or reputable news, because the primary text could not be read in full.
  - **Unconfirmed**: not verified. Do not build hard logic on it; ask.
- **As of 2026-10**: the status at the research date, including pending changes.
- **Applicability tags** in brackets: **[All]** any system processing health data of people in Oman; **[Gov]** Ministry of Health or other government health facilities and their vendors; **[Private]** private hospitals, clinics and other private providers; **[Insurer]** insurers and TPAs; **[Claims]** provider systems handling eligibility, prior approval or claims; **[Telehealth]**; **[AI]**; **[Device]** software that may be a medical device; **[Research]**; **[Prescribing]**; **[Pharmacy]**; **[HIE]** systems exchanging data with national platforms; **[CNI]** critical national infrastructure; **[Cloud]**.

## Requirements

### A. Confidentiality, consent and clinical documentation

#### MOH-01: Patient confidentiality and lawful disclosure [All]
- **Requirement**: Practitioners must not disclose patient secrets learned through their practice without the patient's written consent. The law allows seven exceptions: (1) to competent authorities to prevent or detect a crime; (2) to competent authorities, relatives and companions where the patient has a disease that threatens public health and safety; (3) to competent authorities and first-degree relatives for hereditary diseases; (4) where disclosure is in the interest of the spouse, told to the spouse in person; (5) where the doctor acts as an expert for a court or official investigation, or is called as a witness in criminal proceedings; (6) where an insurer, or another body named by ministerial decision, asked the doctor to examine the patient, limited to that purpose; (7) for the doctor's own defence before an investigating or judicial body. Separately, the Public Health Law makes confidential any health information that identifies the patient and any information about their condition, treatment or care. It may be disclosed only in cases set by laws in force. Breaching Art. 33 is punishable by 1 month to 1 year in prison and/or a fine of OMR 500 to 1,000 (Art. 58).
- **Engineering implication**: Treat all patient-identifiable data as confidential by default. Every disclosure path (export, share, print, API, integration) must record its legal basis: a stored, linked written consent or one of the listed exceptions. Build a disclosure-reason code list that maps to these exceptions and to the policy purposes in MOH-02. Block the disclosure when no basis is recorded.
- **Source**: RD 75/2019 Arts 33 and 58 (Arabic; our translation), https://qanoon.om/p/2019/rd2019075/ ; RD 43/2025 Art. 8, https://qanoon.om/p/2025/rd2025043/ . Al Tamimi (Jan 2020) gives the same summary of Art. 33: https://tamimi.com/news/omans-new-law-on-the-practice-of-medicine-and-allied-health-professions
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: In force.

#### MOH-02: Release-of-information controls [All]
- **Requirement**: The National Policy lists the purposes for which an institution may use or disclose patient information: treatment; billing; education and training; people involved in the patient's care; business partners; contacting the patient; the hospital directory; public health and safety; laws, court orders, security bodies, regulators and investigation of a patient's complaint; work-injury claims; research; and coding review (cl. 8.16). Specific controls apply. Treatment access is limited to the care team when needed (cl. 8.16.1). Billing disclosures go only to the relevant departments, the health insurance platform, claims, revenue-cycle and coding companies, all bound by third-party contracts (cl. 8.16.2). Relatives up to the fourth degree may be told about the patient's condition unless the patient named specific people (cl. 8.16.4). Directory listings show only name and location, and need the patient's consent (cl. 8.16.7). Requests from security bodies, courts, prosecutors, prisons, regulators and complaint investigators need a written request. Access must be time-limited and minimal. The information must be complete, including amendments and deletions. Access is revoked when the investigation ends; the institution may instead send the data through official channels (cl. 8.16.9). DGPHE also requires the written consent of the patient or legal representative to release information from the medical record (2.4.11).
- **Engineering implication**: Build a release-of-information module: request intake, requester identity, purpose code, consent capture, scoped and time-boxed access for external parties, automatic revocation, and exports that include amendment and deletion history. Add a directory opt-in flag. Write every disclosure to the patient's disclosure log (MOH-17).
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.16 to 8.16.12 (Arabic; our translation), URL in Key instruments; DGPHE Medical Record Documentation Guideline 2025, 2.4.11, https://moh.gov.om/media/ofjllkvn/medical-record-documentaion-guideline.pdf
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Policy published May 2024. Scope clause covers government and private health institutions. The Minister's foreword expects any institution processing health data in Oman to apply it. MOH's 2026 plan targets "activation" in private hospitals.

#### MOH-03: Consent to treatment and written-consent procedures [All]
- **Requirement**: No treatment without the patient's consent, or a companion's consent if the patient lacks capacity. Exceptions: emergencies where prior consent cannot be obtained, and infectious diseases or conditions threatening public health or safety (Art. 26). The law requires written consent for: surgery, from the patient, or the spouse or a relative to the second degree if the patient lacks capacity, "on the form prepared by the Ministry", covering the risks and complications, except in emergencies (Art. 37(4) and (5)); research or experiments on humans (Art. 28); assisted reproduction, by both spouses (Art. 29); sterilisation of a woman (Art. 31); and abortion (Art. 36). A patient leaving at their own request must sign a written acknowledgement, recorded in the medical record (Art. 11). DGPHE requires evidence of informed consent in the record (2.1.6, 3.7.1(F), 3.7.2(I), 3.7.8). Penalties: breaches of Arts 26 and 37 fall under Art. 58 (1 month to 1 year and/or OMR 500 to 1,000). Breaches of Arts 28, 29, 31 and 36 fall under Art. 59 (1 to 5 years and/or OMR 3,000 to 10,000).
- **Engineering implication**: Build a consent module whose consent types map to these articles. For surgery, reproduce the MOH surgical consent form content (risks and complications) and record who signed and in what capacity (patient, spouse, relative to the second degree). Record emergency overrides with a reason. Provide a "left against medical advice" (LAMA) form with signature, stored in the record. For e-signatures, meet the Electronic Transactions Law conditions: content kept in its original form, retrievable later, and the originator, date and time identifiable (RD 39/2025 Arts 8 to 10; see NAT-24 and NAT-25). Get adviser confirmation that MOH accepts e-signed versions of its own forms ("the form prepared by the Ministry").
- **Source**: RD 75/2019 Arts 11, 26, 28, 29, 31, 36, 37, 58, 59 (Arabic; our translation); DGPHE guideline 2025, 2.1.6, 3.7.1, 3.7.2, 3.7.8; Electronic Transactions Law RD 39/2025 Arts 8 to 10 (it replaced RD 69/2008 on 14 Apr 2025), https://qanoon.om/p/2025/rd2025039/
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for the consent duties. Unconfirmed whether MOH accepts electronic signatures on its consent forms.
- **As of 2026-10**: In force. The MOH surgical consent form itself was not found online.

#### MOH-04: Consent for clinical photographs and audio or video recordings [All]
- **Requirement**: Photographs and audio or video recordings need a written consent form from the patient or representative. Exceptions: lab slides, X-ray and ultrasound images, endoscopy images, images of internal organs and recordings of internal organ function. Use is limited to the original consent; any new use needs new consent. Permitted purposes: assessment and treatment (stored in the record), evidence after injury or assault, teaching and training, clinical research, and publication. The patient must be told what will be recorded, why, who will see it, how it will be used, whether it can be withdrawn once public, and how copies are kept. The patient may stop the recording or withdraw consent at any time. If consent is refused, the recording must be destroyed as soon as possible. Media is stored in the health record with extra access restrictions (cl. 8.7). Photos and recordings are "highly sensitive" data (cl. 8.3.1).
- **Engineering implication**: Media capture (apps, telehealth, cameras) must check for a valid recording consent of the right scope before capture. Store media in the EHR with purpose tags and tighter role-based access. Provide a withdrawal-and-destroy workflow. Keep separate consent scopes for treatment, teaching, research and publication.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.3.1 and 8.7 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published policy.

#### MOH-05: Minimum clinical documentation content [All]
- **Requirement**: Practitioners must record the patient's condition, diagnosis and prescribed treatment in the designated record (Art. 10(5)). When the patient or a relative is told about the diagnosis, this must be recorded (Art. 12). DGPHE sets the minimum content. Registration face sheet: file number, full name, gender, address, date of birth or age, contact details, allergies (3.1). Outpatient visit: date, history, findings, diagnosis, investigations, plan, results, missed appointments, medications (3.2). Inpatient: admission date and time, identity data, history within 24 hours of admission, assessment, diagnoses, care plan, consults, evidence of informed consent, reports, discharge summary (3.7). Operative reports are authenticated within 24 hours (2.7.4). Records may be paper, electronic or both, and are kept for every patient, including newborns (2.1.9). Facilities keep indices (master patient index, diagnostic, operation) and registers (patient, operation, birth and death) (2.1.10, 2.1.11). The licensing manual requires a unique identifier with an alert for patients of the same name, and medical reports that are typed (not handwritten), signed, stamped, timed and dated. Non-compliance can lead to a warning, financial penalties, suspension of a physician for up to 1 year, closure for up to 60 days, or licence cancellation (DGPHE 5.4).
- **Engineering implication**: Configure EMR templates with these mandatory fields and completion checks, for example history within 24 hours of admission and operative report within 24 hours. Provide the master patient index, diagnostic and operation indices and the registers. Generate typed, signed medical reports with date, time and a facility stamp equivalent. Flag patients who share a name.
- **Source**: RD 75/2019 Arts 10(5) and 12 (Arabic; our translation); DGPHE guideline 2025, 2.1.9 to 2.1.11, 2.7.4, 3.1 to 3.8, 5.4; Licensing Manual 2024, Domain Three (pp. 49 to 51), https://moh.gov.om/media/ootl0qmv/licensing-regulations-for-private-health-establishment-manual-1-1.pdf
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: The March 2025 guideline replaces Policy P003-2012 for the private sector. An equivalent documentation policy for MOH (government) facilities was not reviewed.

#### MOH-06: Record integrity: authorship, amendments, deletions, copy-paste and merges [All]
- **Requirement**: Entries must be dated and legible, with the author identified by name and designation and authenticated (computer entries must be verifiable). Electronic records must trace every change with the person and time (DGPHE 2.1.12, 2.1.14). Verbal or telephone orders are recorded at once, read back, and authenticated within 24 hours (2.1.13). Section 2.6 of the same guideline says 48 hours. Blanket orders such as "continue previous medications" are prohibited (2.6). The National Policy adds: document only under your own username, in the correct record, at the time of care; the team head countersigns where needed (cl. 8.5.1). Addenda outside an open visit are for limited cases, need a reason and are tracked (cl. 8.5.2). Copy-paste is allowed only within the same patient's record and never from outside sources. A tool must show the source, user and date of copied text (cl. 8.10). Deletion is allowed only for registration errors, documentation errors or mixed-up records. Deleted content stays visible as red strikethrough with the reason, user, date and time, and appears on printouts. Deletion reports are kept and the patient is told when needed (cl. 8.11). The clause also says data already saved in the record should not be deleted; the wording is unclear. Duplicate records are merged only by health information management (HIM) staff, with traceability (cl. 8.12). Scanned paper is linked to the electronic record, then the paper is disposed of securely (cl. 8.9).
- **Engineering implication**: Keep an immutable audit of every create, update and delete with user, role and timestamp. No hard deletes: use logical deletion shown as red strikethrough, with a mandatory reason, and include it in prints. Track copy-paste provenance. Add an addendum entry type with a mandatory reason. Build a verbal-order workflow with a read-back flag and a countersignature timer (configure 24 hours pending adviser view). Support patient-record merge and unmerge with full lineage.
- **Source**: DGPHE guideline 2025, 2.1.12 to 2.1.14, 2.6; National Policy cl. 8.5, 8.9 to 8.12 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: The 24-hour versus 48-hour conflict inside the DGPHE guideline is unresolved.

### B. Patient identity, data classification and access

#### MOH-07: Patient registration and national identifier [All] [HIE]
- **Requirement**: Register patients using the ID card (Omanis) or resident card (residents), and check that the card matches the person. Prevent duplicate records. Minimum demographics: civil number or resident number; first, second and third names and tribe for Omanis; first and second names for residents; date of birth; nationality; sex; marital status; place of residence; phone; employer; occupation; religion; the name and phone number of a relative. Update and check demographics at every visit (cl. 8.4). Patients may update demographics at registration by showing their civil card (cl. 9.7). The national health record identifies patients by civil number and resident number (cl. 8.15(2)). MOH's IT directorate describes its national e-health record as using the civil ID number as the unique identifier. MOH's e-referral system blocks non-urgent referrals when the patient file has no civil ID number (Referral SOP 5.1.29).
- **Engineering implication**: Make the civil or resident number a required, validated identifier with a uniqueness check. Support ID card reading where available. Store name parts separately, including tribe. Run duplicate detection at registration. Handle newborns, visitors without IDs and downtime with temporary identifiers and a later reconciliation workflow (see MOH-11).
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.4, 8.15, 9.7 (Arabic; our translation); MOH DG of IT and Digital Health page, https://moh.gov.om/en/hospitals-directorates/directorates-and-centers-at-hq/directorate-general-of-information-technology-and-digital-health/ ; Procedures of Patient Referral 5.1.29; Licensing Manual 2024, Domain Three.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: In use. Mandatory religion and tribe fields may raise PDPL sensitive-data questions; that analysis belongs to the PDPL research.

#### MOH-08: Health data classification and masking [All]
- **Requirement**: Classify all health data, in any format and location, into three levels (cl. 8.3). Highly confidential: personal data on physical, mental and psychological health. Confidential: identifiers such as civil number, resident number, health record number, phone, social security data and card numbers; authentication data; financial records; email. Public (open): services offered, specialist staff available and general statistics. Highly confidential data has two grades. "Highly sensitive": psychiatric illness, infectious diseases, addiction, assaults, crimes, suicide, photographs, audio or video recordings and genetic data. "Sensitive": all other record contents. Only the care team that documented the data may access highly confidential data, on a minimum-necessary basis. Institutions must be able to mask record data, fully or partly, for social, psychological, security or political reasons; mask identifiers when data is used for research or other purposes; and hide inpatients from the hospital directory on request (cl. 8.13). Sensitive data in databases must be encrypted according to its classification (cl. 18.1(5)). DGPHE allows very confidential parts of a record (for example some psychiatric treatment) to be stored separately, with a marker in the main record (2.4.15).
- **Engineering implication**: Tag data at field or document level with its classification and sensitivity category (psychiatry, infectious disease, addiction, assault, suicide, media, genetics). Enforce care-team and role-based access. Provide a masking or sealing feature with a reason, a visible "data withheld" marker and break-glass override (MOH-10). Use field-level encryption for highly sensitive data.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.3, 8.13, 18.1 (Arabic; our translation); DGPHE guideline 2025, 2.4.15.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published policy.

#### MOH-09: User accounts, authentication and least privilege [All]
- **Requirement**: Staff, temporary users and trainees must sign a confidentiality undertaking before system access (cl. 7.1). The login page must warn that unauthorised use is prohibited and that violators face legal action (cl. 7.2(4)). Each user gets a unique ID; no IDs named after departments; all login IDs are reviewed or changed at least twice a year; accounts lock after no more than 3 failed logins; IDs inactive for more than 180 days are disabled; the same ID may not be logged in on more than one device at once (cl. 7.3). Passwords: change the initial password at first login; at least 8 characters; upper and lower case, digits and special characters; change every 180 days; change at once if compromised; no reuse within 1 year; masked on screen; a different password for each application (cl. 7.4). HIM and IT set privileges by job role on a least-privilege basis. Each system needs a privilege administration page. Privileges are reviewed every year (cl. 7.5). Accounts are frozen during leave and closed on leaving, ideally automatically through a link to the HR system. Managers receive active-account lists twice a year (cl. 7.6, 7.7). Trainee accounts close automatically when training ends (cl. 8.16.3). Remote-work devices lock after 15 minutes of inactivity (cl. 10.3(6)). Staff complete privacy, security, malware and password training (cl. 5.2, 26).
- **Engineering implication**: Configure identity and access management to match: lockout at 3 attempts or fewer, disable after 180 days inactive, single active session, the password rules above, a login banner, a user-privilege admin console, HR-driven joiner, mover and leaver processing, scheduled access reviews, time-bound trainee accounts, and e-signature of the confidentiality undertaking at first login. If you use multi-factor authentication instead of 180-day password expiry, record the deviation and get adviser sign-off.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 5.2, 7.1 to 7.7, 8.16.3, 10.3, 26 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published policy. Some rules (180-day expiry, no concurrent sessions) differ from current international practice.

#### MOH-10: Audit trails, activity review and break-glass access [All]
- **Requirement**: Keep audit logs on all computers and servers that process, send or store health data. Each access attempt must log at least: user ID, login date and time, device used, and the scope of patient data accessed. Each system needs a control panel to track user activity. Audit trails are stored on a separate server. Use intrusion detection. Audit outputs such as printing periodically. Keep a documented list of user privileges (cl. 16.2). Review system activity at least yearly, or when misuse is suspected, including failed logins, lockouts, unauthorised access attempts and accounts no longer needed. IT runs a full security scan twice a year (cl. 17). Printing is limited to the treating doctor and HIM staff, to listed documents, at the patient's request. Every printed page carries the printing user's ID, and printing is tracked (cl. 8.14). Break-glass: in an emergency the user submits an automatic request stating the reason. The log records at least user name, job title, reason, date and time, and any additions, edits or deletions. HIM reviews whether each use was justified. Break-glass logs are kept for at least 5 years. Extra controls apply to highly sensitive or masked data (cl. 22, 8.16.1(2)). Institutions appoint a health data protection officer from among HIM specialists (cl. 5.1).
- **Engineering implication**: Build a central, tamper-evident audit store, separate from the application database. Log reads as well as writes, with the patient scope. Provide an activity dashboard for the HIM unit and the data protection officer. Watermark prints with the user ID and keep a print log. Build a break-glass flow with reason codes, alerts to HIM, a review queue and log retention of at least 5 years.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 5.1, 8.14, 8.16.1, 16, 17, 22 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published policy. No general audit-log retention period was found apart from the 5-year break-glass minimum.

### C. Security, incidents, vendors and hosting

#### MOH-11: Encryption, integrity, change control, downtime and recovery [All]
- **Requirement**: Encrypt data and files before sending them between institutions. The two parties agree how to manage keys, install certificates on email and use approved file-transfer protocols (cl. 14). Change management: log every change, back up before changing, check backups, be able to roll back, and tell users about major changes (cl. 15). Integrity: monitor all changes including deletions, use intrusion detection, test systems before use and apply fixes (cl. 18). HIM, with IT, keeps an emergency and disaster recovery plan, with copies on and off site and paper forms ready. An emergency response team meets yearly and tests the plan (cl. 19). Downtime: IT tells HIM about planned and unplanned outages and recovery. HIM prepares temporary patient numbers in advance and paper records, and staff enter the data into the EHR once it is back (cl. 21). Teleradiology providers need a data and disaster recovery policy (Teleradiology 4.4.5).
- **Engineering implication**: Use TLS for all transport and encrypted file exchange between institutions with agreed keys. Run releases through a change log, pre-change backups and tested rollback. Provide a downtime kit: printable forms, pre-allocated temporary record numbers and a reconciliation workflow. Keep a disaster recovery plan with evidence of a yearly test.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 14, 15, 18, 19, 21 (Arabic; our translation); Teleradiology Guideline 4.4.5.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published policy. No recovery time or recovery point targets are set.

#### MOH-12: Breach and privacy-incident reporting [All]
- **Requirement**: Staff and business partners must report any possible breach to the HIM unit within 24 hours, with as much detail as possible (cl. 23.1). HIM tells top management at once (cl. 23.3). Top management tells the Ministry of Transport, Communications and IT (MTCIT) and the Cyber Defence Centre (CDC) at once. The CDC report covers the data affected, the responsible staff member's name and phone, expected consequences and measures taken (cl. 23.4, 23.5). Containment: stop the unauthorised practice, restore data where possible, and shut down the breached system (cl. 23.6). Affected patients are told within 10 working days. Clause 9.13 counts from the date of the breach; clause 23.7(3) counts from the end of the investigation and risk assessment. The patient notice covers all breaches of their data, the nature of the data, the measures taken and a contact point (cl. 9.13). For privacy violations, record the details, suspend the violator's account pending investigation and report to the MOH unit responsible for information privacy and security (cl. 24).
- **Engineering implication**: Write an incident-response runbook with these clocks. Vendor contracts must require notice to the client within 24 hours. Prepare notice templates for MTCIT, the CDC and patients. Make sure audit logs can quickly identify which patients were affected. Provide an account-suspension tool.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 6.6, 9.13, 23, 24 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary). The start point of the 10-day patient notice is inconsistent within the policy.
- **As of 2026-10**: PDPL breach rules apply in parallel; see the PDPL research.

#### MOH-13: Vendors, contractors and remote access [All]
- **Requirement**: The policy's definition of "employee" includes staff of companies delivering projects, trainees, volunteers and anyone who uses the institution's systems (Definitions, item 3). Vendor staff are therefore covered by the confidentiality undertaking, training and access rules. Before giving a third party access, the institution must review the contract to make sure it covers 17 points (cl. 10.6). These include: security standards; a risk assessment; the right to audit; incident reporting and investigation; a description of each service; least access; a detailed, auditable list of users; the identity and number of third-party staff; service hours; protection and audit procedures; the right to monitor and cancel user activity; limits on copying and disclosure; installation and maintenance duties; destruction of software and data held by the contractor at the end of the contract; physical protection; training; and a list of security measures agreed before signing. IT must risk-assess any connection to a third-party site (cl. 10.5). All external data transfers must rest on a formal contract, a non-disclosure agreement or a business partnership agreement (cl. 10.4(9)). Remote access needs prior approval and an approved VPN, and IT may end any unauthorised session without notice (cl. 10.4). Software and documents produced by staff or contractors belong to the institution unless a contract says otherwise. Developers sign an acknowledgement of this when appointed (cl. 11.3).
- **Engineering implication**: Prepare a vendor onboarding pack: a signed confidentiality undertaking per person, a named-user list, training records, VPN-only remote support with session termination, a contract schedule that maps to the 17 points in cl. 10.6, and a data-destruction certificate at exit. Set out IP ownership explicitly in the contract, because the default favours the institution.
- **Source**: National Policy Definitions item 3; cl. 7.1, 8.16.5, 10.2 to 10.6, 11.3, 26 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published policy.

#### MOH-14: Cloud hosting and processing outside Oman [All]
- **Requirement**: Institutions may use cloud services if they meet data classification, security, administrative and out-of-country requirements (cl. 13.1 to 13.3). Encrypt data hosted, transmitted and processed in the cloud. Do not host or process health data in the cloud unless security and privacy controls are in place and monitored, as shown by a risk assessment, data classification and contract terms (cl. 13.5). Use only cloud providers approved by MTCIT. Do not store health data on external servers without a valid contract. Contracts must bind the provider and any subcontractors, whether in Oman or abroad (where an exception for abroad is granted), and must allow exit and the return of all data in formats the institution approves. Legal counsel reviews the service level agreement (cl. 13.6). Hosting data or applications outside Oman needs all of the following: no alternative available inside Oman; prior approval from the Cyber Defence Centre, on a reasoned request, before storing or processing any health data outside Oman; the request limited to software as a service (SaaS); an internal risk assessment; compliance with MTCIT and CDC rules; and a service level agreement reviewed by legal counsel. Private institutions that handle government data also need approval from the sector regulator, such as the Central Bank of Oman or the TRA (cl. 13.7). Other MOH documents add in-country rules for telehealth apps (TEL-02), teleradiology (TEL-08) and imaging AI (MOH-26).
- **Engineering implication**: Default to in-country hosting with an MTCIT-approved provider. Treat any non-Omani cloud region, SaaS sub-processor, large language model API, analytics tool or support tool that sees health data as processing outside Oman. That needs CDC approval and is only possible for SaaS. Keep a data-residency register. Block cross-border replication, backups and telemetry that contain health data unless approved.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 13.1 to 13.7 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published policy. MTCIT cloud rules and CDC procedures are covered by the cloud and cybersecurity research. Whether the CDC runs a working approval route for health SaaS is unconfirmed.

### D. Retention and patient rights

#### MOH-15: Retention, archiving, destruction and closure [All]
- **Requirement**: Institutions follow the retention schedule approved by the National Records and Archives Authority (NRAA) and the Documents and Archives Law and its regulation (cl. 8.19, 8.19.1(1)). A health record is a "current" document while the patient is still being treated. It becomes an "intermediate" document after the patient has had no treatment for more than 10 years, or 10 years after death. It is archived after the intermediate period. Selective archiving of components is allowed, with fast retrieval and a marker in the record showing what was archived (cl. 8.19.1). On closure, an institution hands all its health records to MOH (cl. 8.19.1(10)). After a death, any outstanding documentation is completed within 10 days; the record is then frozen against further procedural actions, and frozen at national level (cl. 8.18). Media and devices are wiped, reset or destroyed under NRAA procedures (cl. 8.19.2). DGPHE requires a destruction log listing each record (patient name and number, dates of service, destruction date, company, witness signatures, method), kept permanently. Shredding or pulping is recommended; burning and burial are not (4.4). The licensing manual points to the NRAA Retention and Destruction Process (2018). Teleradiology images and reports are kept under the NRAA retention policy; the referring centre is responsible (Teleradiology 4.2.4, 4.2.5).
- **Engineering implication**: Model retention states (active, current, intermediate, archived, frozen) with the 10-year triggers. Do not auto-delete until the NRAA period is confirmed. Build a freeze-on-death workflow with a 10-day window. Archive with fast retrieval and integrity checks. Provide a bulk export for handover to MOH on closure. Keep a permanent destruction log and certified media sanitisation.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.18, 8.19 (Arabic; our translation); DGPHE guideline 2025, section 4; Licensing Manual 2024, Domain Three; Teleradiology Guideline 4.2.4, 4.2.5.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for these rules. The actual NRAA retention periods for health records are Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: The DGPHE guideline refers to a retention schedule, but neither the 2012 nor the 2025 published version contains one.

#### MOH-16: Patient access to records and reports [All]
- **Requirement**: Patients may get a copy of their key health information: discharge summary, lab results, medication list, diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, and immunisation record (cl. 9.2). A detailed medical report must be decided and delivered within 15 working days; any refusal must give reasons (cl. 9.3). Patients may move their data to another institution, or to another doctor in the same institution (cl. 9.10). Imaging and video data can be downloaded through HIM at the patient's request; fees may apply (cl. 8.8.2). All telehealth medical documents must be available to the patient (cl. 8.6.2.2(9)). DGPHE: records are available to the patient or representative through the attending professional at reasonable times (2.1.8); the discharge report is given free on discharge (3.7.4). The licensing manual gives patients the right to a copy of their personal medical records. The MOH patient rights document lists the right to a discharge summary and a medical report.
- **Engineering implication**: Provide a patient portal or HIM workflow for record copies and medical reports, with a 15-working-day timer, reasoned-refusal capture and fee handling. Offer export as PDF and in a machine-readable format (for example FHIR) for portability. Generate the discharge summary at discharge at no charge.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.6.2.2(9), 8.8.2, 9.2, 9.3, 9.10 (Arabic; our translation); DGPHE guideline 2025, 2.1.8, 3.7.4; Licensing Manual 2024, Domain Four; National Document of Patient Rights and Responsibilities, https://moh.gov.om/media/hjxfqpoj/d8b62e21-161e-03b9-0517-6e57c065b8c1.pdf
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Published.

#### MOH-17: Patient rights: correction, restriction, disclosure log and complaints [All]
- **Requirement**: Patients may ask for incorrect data to be corrected or erased. The request is decided within 15 working days and may be refused only on listed grounds (cl. 9.4 to 9.6). Patients may ask for their data to be withheld or its use limited, including sharing to the national health record. This is decided within 15 days, with exceptions for emergency treatment, uses that need no consent, and court or security orders (cl. 9.11, 9.12). Patients may get a list of disclosures covering up to one calendar year before the request, within 15 days, showing date, recipient, type of data and reason, with listed exclusions such as treatment, payment and disclosures the patient asked for (cl. 9.8, 9.9). Complaints that processing breaches the PDPL are decided within 15 working days; silence counts as rejection; the patient may appeal to management within 15 days (cl. 9.14). Clause 9.1 says personal data in health systems belongs to the State and is processed transparently, honestly, with respect for human dignity, and "after written, clear, explicit and understandable consent" from the person. Private facilities must display a Charter of Patients' Rights and Responsibilities in Arabic and English in care and waiting areas and on their website, disclose costs, and run a complaints programme (Licensing Manual, Domain Four). The MOH patient rights document covers privacy during and after care, the right to refuse or withdraw from research, and complaints to the head of the health centre or the hospital patient services department.
- **Engineering implication**: Build request-tracking workflows with timers for correction, restriction and disclosure-log requests. Add a patient-level "do not share to the national record" flag that the integration engine honours, with logged emergency override. Build a disclosure-log report. Add a complaints module. Publish an Arabic and English patient-rights page in portals and apps.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 9.1, 9.4 to 9.9, 9.11 to 9.14 (Arabic; our translation); Licensing Manual 2024, Domain Four; National Document of Patient Rights and Responsibilities; RD 43/2025 Art. 6.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary). What the consent wording in cl. 9.1 means in practice is Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: Published.

### E. National platforms, licensing and reporting

#### MOH-18: National Health Record sharing (Shifa) [HIE] [Private] [Gov]
- **Requirement**: Health institutions must share their data with related institutions through the national health record, to ensure digital integration (cl. 8.15.1). When sharing, they must: tell the patient; identify the patient by civil number or resident number; provide the technical tools to link their records to the national record; and share only the minimum data the national record needs (cl. 8.15). Sharing must be for lawful purposes, on a justified need-to-know basis, under data sharing agreements and after weighing the risks to the patient (cl. 8.15.2). Sharing between government bodies needs a benefit and risk assessment, records of sharing decisions, PDPL compliance and no onward sharing without the data owner's consent (cl. 8.15.3). Data quality principles apply (cl. 8.15.4). MOH reports that in 2025 its national health record project (the Shifa application) achieved "integration with private healthcare institutions via FHIR standards". Its 2026 to 2027 targets include "enhanced integration with private hospitals" and "expanded implementation of FHIR standards". MOH's IT directorate says Al-Shifa runs in more than 200 institutions and that the national e-health record links public and private institutions using the civil ID. The Shifa app page calls the app the "community gateway for the National Unified Health Record project". It lists expansion to private institutions as a phased future goal and offers login by ID card or Mobile PKI.
- **Engineering implication**: Design for FHIR-based exchange with the MOH national record: patient matching on civil or resident number, mapping to the minimum dataset, a patient notification flag, the patient opt-out from MOH-17, and a data sharing agreement. Before building, ask MOH's IT directorate for the FHIR implementation guide, profiles, terminology bindings, onboarding steps and test environment. Log every submission and retrieval.

- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for the policy duty and MOH statements. FHIR profiles, scope and deadlines are Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: No deadline for private connection found. On 26 Apr 2026 the Minister told the Majlis A'Shura that Shifa will become an independent government technical entity under the Oman Investment Authority (Oman Observer; secondary). A 2025 peer-reviewed paper found no unified data exchange across institutions yet (EMHJ; secondary).

#### MOH-19: Private facility duties: MOH-approved information system, inspector access, statistics and coding [Private]
- **Requirement**: To obtain a licence, a private facility must submit evidence that MOH has approved its electronic health information system. This is due within 12 months of initial approval, or the approval lapses (MD 231/2024 Art. 8(4)). Private facilities must provide an electronic health information system "in accordance with the systems approved by the Ministry" (Art. 29). Facilities and their staff must help MOH staff during supervision and let them see records and data on request (Art. 30). The DGPHE supervision policy says inspectors may view and copy paper and electronic files, records and data, and that obstruction leads to legal and administrative action. Facility licences last 3 years (Art. 11). Statistics: each quarter, report to DGPHE and the governorate patients by ICD-10 code, nationality, gender and age group, plus dental treatments, traditional and complementary medicine visits, staff, lab tests, radiology procedures and immunisations (Licensing Manual). Also submit the monthly and annual statistical report in MOH's booklet format. Qualified coders code records using the latest ICD revision, and registers and diagnostic indexes follow ICD-10 (DGPHE 2.2.6, 2.3). Sanctions on a private institution: warning; fine of OMR 500 to 5,000; closure for up to 1 year; licence cancellation (RD 75/2019 Art. 52).
- **Engineering implication**: A private-facility EMR must be approvable by MOH. Prepare a compliance dossier mapped to these notes and to the National Policy, and engage DGPHE and MOH's IT directorate early. Provide a read-only, time-boxed, audited inspector role and on-demand record export. Build ICD-10 coding and the quarterly statistics report with DGPHE's breakdowns. Plan for a future ICD version change.

- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for the duties. The approval criteria and process are Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: In force since 9 Sep 2024. No published list of approved systems or approval checklist was found.

#### MOH-20: Mandatory notifications [All]
- **Requirement**: Communicable diseases: report a case or suspected case to the nearest health institution, on the MOH form, within 24 hours (first-section diseases), one week (second section) or 20 days (third section). The duty falls in order on the examining doctor, the head of the institution, the lab head and others. The report must include the three-part name, tribe or family name, residence and work (RD 73/92 Arts 2 to 4). MOH's 2017 manual: Group A within 24 hours by the fastest means to the governorate and national surveillance units; Group B within one week. Private institutions also send a monthly communicable disease report. In Al-Shifa 3Plus, the e-notification form opens automatically when a notifiable ICD code is entered. The Public Health Law has MOH set the disease list and a comprehensive surveillance and reporting system, including registers (Arts 25, 27). Tarassud is MOH's electronic surveillance platform. It launched in 2017, is integrated with Al-Shifa, and was opened to the private sector during COVID-19. Rassed is an MOH app for field surveillance data. Births and deaths: Ajyal notifies civil status centres of births and deaths in hospitals. Private facilities must tell MOH and the nearest police station about any criminal incident, or any death of a patient or companion, and keep a special register of these cases (MD 231/2024 Art. 34). All abortions are reported to MOH on the set form (Art. 41). The National Policy permits disclosure for these purposes (cl. 8.16.8).
- **Engineering implication**: Build a notifiable-disease rules engine driven by ICD codes and lab results. It should raise alerts, pre-fill notifications with the required fields, run timers by disease group and keep an audit trail. Confirm the technical channel for private facilities (Tarassud access, Al-Shifa or a manual form). Add birth and death notification workflows (Ajyal or manual), a death and criminal-incident register, and an abortion report form.
- **Source**: RD 73/92 Arts 2 to 4 (Arabic; our translation); RD 43/2025 Arts 25, 27; Communicable Diseases Manual 2017, §3.6 and private-sector section; MD 231/2024 Arts 34, 41; National Policy cl. 8.16.8; MOH Ajyal page, https://moh.gov.om/en/services/employees/internal-systems/ajyal-system/ ; MOH Rassed page, https://moh.gov.om/en/services/employees/rassed-system/rassed-system/ ; Al Wahaibi et al. 2026 (Tarassud), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44197-026-00556-5
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for the laws, MD and MOH pages. Tarassud history is Confirmed (secondary).
- **As of 2026-10**: The electronic channel for private-sector notification is Unconfirmed. Statutory deadlines for birth and death registration under civil status law were not researched.

#### MOH-21: Practitioner licensing, privileges and visiting practitioners [All]
- **Requirement**: No one may practise without an MOH licence and malpractice cover: the compensation fund in government institutions, or third-party liability insurance in private ones (RD 75/2019 Art. 7). Professional titles may be used only by registered, licensed practitioners (MD 231/2024 Art. 13). Licence conditions include passing the set professional assessment (Art. 17(13)). The Oman Medical Specialty Board (OMSB) says candidates who want to work for MOH or other health institutions must sit its Occupational Classification exams. A licence comes with listed clinical privileges. It lasts 2 years for private practice and 3 years for government (Art. 20). Renewal is tied to a point 30 days before expiry; the Arabic does not make clear whether that is a deadline or a window (Art. 21). Practitioners must practise within their privileges (Art. 36). Private facilities may not employ unlicensed practitioners, and practitioners may not work at another institution without MOH approval (Art. 32). Visiting practitioners need a valid licence where they work, the required qualifications, a pass in the visiting assessment, a clean record and liability insurance. The facility applies, MOH decides within 7 working days, and the licence lasts up to 3 months (Arts 23 to 26). Penalties: practising without a licence falls under RD 75/2019 Art. 58; false documents to obtain a licence carry 6 months to 3 years and/or OMR 1,000 to 3,000 (Art. 57).
- **Engineering implication**: Keep a credential register: licence number, category, clinical privileges, expiry, licensed facility and visiting-licence dates. Block orders, prescriptions, signatures and telehealth sessions by users without a valid licence or acting outside their privileges. Alert at least 30 days before expiry. Print the licence number on prescriptions, sick leave, referrals and reports (MOH-22).
- **Source**: RD 75/2019 Arts 7, 8, 57, 58 (Arabic; our translation); MD 231/2024 Arts 13, 17, 20, 21, 23 to 26, 32, 36 (Arabic); OMSB exams page, https://omsb.gov.om/home/infoPage?id=587
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: In force. Whether MOH offers a licence-verification API is Unconfirmed.

#### MOH-22: Prescriptions, sick-leave certificates and medical reports [Prescribing] [All]
- **Requirement**: A prescription must state the treatment, quantity, duration and method of use, clearly and in writing, with the prescriber's name, signature and date (RD 75/2019 Art. 10(6)). No prescribing before examining the patient, except in necessity (Art. 18). False reports or certificates and unjustified sick leave are banned (Art. 19, penalised under Art. 58). DGPHE prescription content: clinic name, address and licence number; doctor's name, licence number and specialty; drug name and form; strength; dosage; route; duration (3.4). Medication orders include date and time, drug, dose, route, frequency, the patient's age and weight, known allergies, the reason and the prescriber. They use metric units, spell out "units", use a leading zero and no trailing zero, and avoid abbreviated drug names (2.5, 2.6). The DGPHE sick-leave certificate content includes "diagnosis including reason for granting the sick leave" (3.5). The National Policy, however, forbids naming the disease on a sick-leave certificate unless the patient agrees or a law requires it (cl. 8.5.1(5)). An MOH e-service lets private-facility doctors issue approved sick leave through the e-health portal; the doctor and clinic licences must be valid. Telehealth sick leave can be downloaded from the platform or sent to the patient (Telehealth Guideline 7.2.5.5).
- **Engineering implication**: Build structured e-prescriptions with mandatory fields and dose-format validation. Print the prescriber's and the facility's licence numbers automatically. In the sick-leave template, hide the diagnosis by default unless consent is recorded, until an adviser resolves the conflict. Look into integration with the MOH e-health portal sick-leave service. No national e-prescription exchange for the private sector was found. The insurance platform Dhamani's e-prescription flows belong to the insurance research.
- **Source**: RD 75/2019 Arts 10(6), 18, 19, 58 (Arabic; our translation); DGPHE guideline 2025, 2.5, 2.6, 3.4, 3.5; National Policy cl. 8.5.1(5); MOH sick-leave e-service, https://moh.gov.om/en/services/businesses/private-health-establishments-dgphe/get-eservice-sick-leave-approval/ ; Telehealth Guideline 7.2.5.5.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary). Whether issuing sick leave through the portal is mandatory is Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: In June 2026 Dhamani reported 271 registered pharmacies and about 60% pharmacy connectivity (The Arabian Stories; secondary).

#### MOH-23: Electronic referrals to and from MOH facilities [HIE] [Private] [Gov]
- **Requirement**: All referrals and back-referrals to and from MOH institutions must be electronic, including those from and to non-MOH government and private facilities (5.1.28). For non-urgent referrals the system blocks sending if the patient file has no civil ID number (5.1.29). The referral form must be complete, clear and in English, with the patient's condition, findings, results, treatment and the exact reason for referral (5.1.27). Consent for a referred procedure is taken at the receiving facility (5.1.26). Virtual clinics may follow accepted referrals (5.5). DGPHE referral content includes licence numbers and the referral type (routine, urgent, emergency) (3.6). The law requires a report of initial findings and treatment with any referral (RD 75/2019 Art. 16).

- **As of 2026-10**: The SOP's review date (Nov 2024) has passed, but it is still on the MOH site. The technical route for private facilities is Unconfirmed.

### F. Research, AI and advertising

#### MOH-24: Research approval, consent and de-identification [Research]
- **Requirement**: Research or experiments on humans need the person's written consent and must follow conditions set by ministerial decision (RD 75/2019 Art. 28; breaches under Art. 59). The Public Health Law repeats the written-consent rule (Art. 12). Disclosure for research needs a written request stating the type and volume of data. Access is limited to the minimum needed. Patient identity is hidden, and consent is needed if researchers must see identifiers. Researchers may not publish genetic or "big" data that could point to the individual or harm the State's reputation. Access is revoked when the study ends (cl. 8.16.11). De-identification means removing name, medical record number, phone, fax, email, civil number, photograph, home address and every date linked directly to the person, such as birth, marriage and death (cl. 6.10). Identifiers are masked when data is used for research (cl. 8.13(2)). Research in MOH institutions needs protocol approval through the National Center for Health Research online system (the Health Studies and Research Approval Committee; its guideline also names the Research and Ethical Review and Approval Committee and regional committees). Non-Omani or non-resident researchers need written approval from the Undersecretary for Planning and an MOH employee as local supervisor. Patients may refuse or withdraw from research without affecting their care (patient rights document).
- **Engineering implication**: Build a research extraction pipeline that de-identifies by default, removing the policy's identifier list including all person-linked dates (consider date shifting). Log requests and approvals, enforce scope and expiry, and block identifiable extracts without consent evidence. Store the ethics approval reference with every dataset. No export abroad without approvals (MOH-14).
- **Source**: RD 75/2019 Arts 28, 59 (Arabic; our translation); RD 43/2025 Art. 12; National Policy cl. 6.10, 8.13(2), 8.16.11; MOH National Center for Health Research, https://moh.gov.om/en/hospitals-directorates/directorates-and-centers-at-hq/national-center-for-health-research/ ; Oman government service page, https://gov.om/en/w/submit-research-proposal-for-approval
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for the law, policy and MOH pages. The ministerial decision required by Art. 28 was not found, and the committees' legal basis is Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: No research-specific ministerial decision found. Rules for research by private institutions outside MOH facilities are unclear.

#### MOH-25: AI system governance [AI]
- **Requirement**: Institutions may use AI for diagnosis, treatment planning, drug development, patient monitoring, personalised medicine and analysis of electronic health records (cl. 12.1). When they do, they must (cl. 12.2):
  - follow the competent bodies' rules on data classification, data protection, security, open data and continuity, as well as Omani law and human-rights commitments;
  - set internal AI rules and document roles and authority through every development stage;
  - make AI accessible to people with disabilities;
  - assess AI risks (security, financial, health, environmental) and protect against attacks such as algorithm manipulation;
  - keep bias out of design and training, audit data collection, algorithms and AI-supported decisions internally, and check input data quality continuously;
  - classify decisions as automated or not, and set the level of human involvement; AI must not take important decisions for people without their prior authorisation;
  - make AI decisions explainable;
  - test AI in an isolated test environment for set periods before production, then monitor and log all AI activity in production;
  - tell everyone affected that AI is in use, and get prior consent where significant automated decisions affect them directly;
  - set quality measures and keep a continuity plan.

  Separately, RD 75/2019 Art. 17 bans "unlicensed means or methods" in diagnosis, treatment or rehabilitation (penalised under Art. 58). How that applies to AI tools is untested. MTCIT's Public Policy for the Safe and Ethical Use of AI Systems (in force 9 Apr 2025; a non-binding circular, according to decree.om) is probably the "competent body" framework.
- **Engineering implication**: Keep an AI register for each deployment: intended use, decision type (automated or assistive), human-in-the-loop design, model documentation, bias testing, validation results from a separate test environment, a monitoring dashboard, production logs of inputs, outputs and overrides, patient-facing AI disclosure, consent capture for significant automated decisions, a rollback and continuity plan, and accessibility.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 12 (Arabic; our translation); RD 75/2019 Arts 17, 58; decree.om note on the MTCIT AI policy, https://blog.decree.om/2025/mtcit-issues-public-policy-for-ethical-use-of-ai/
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for cl. 12. How Art. 17 applies to AI is Unconfirmed. The MTCIT policy details are Confirmed (secondary).
- **As of 2026-10**: MOH plans AI decision support and predictive analytics for 2026 to 2027 (Press Statement, Feb 2026).

#### MOH-26: AI in diagnostic imaging [AI] [Private] [Gov]
- **Requirement**: The DGPHE AI-in-radiology guideline sets these rules:
  - **Hosting and data**: host the AI and every associated system locally, at MOH or in MOH-approved facilities (4.1.5). Do not share or send any data outside Oman for any purpose (4.1.6). Train models on locally hosted servers using Omani population data (4.1.7). Keep all servers and storage inside Oman (4.5.2).
  - **Privacy**: anonymise data by design, with an option to store no patient data at all (4.1.8). Encrypt data in transit and at rest (4.4.2). De-identify training data (4.4.4).
  - **Standards and integration**: report results in standard units such as BI-RADS and Gleason scores (4.1.9). Use DICOM, HL7/FHIR and web APIs, and integrate with AlShifa and other MOH systems (4.1.10, 4.2.4).
  - **Approval**: tools must hold recognised regulatory approval; the guideline gives FDA and CE as examples ("like FDA …, CE … etc.", 4.2.2, 4.5.1). Which approvals MOH accepts is a question for MOH. AI vendors apply to MOH for integration testing with AlShifa and MOH systems. MOH's Digital Transformation Department, IT directorate and Radiology Services Committee assess them (4.3.1, 4.3.2).
  - **Clinical responsibility**: AI gives primary readings only. A competent radiologist prepares and validates the final report and carries the legal responsibility; liability is agreed in writing before the project starts (4.3.3, 4.3.4, Annex).
  - **Monitoring**: monitor performance, log AI decisions, inputs, outcomes and clinician overrides (4.5.6), and revalidate every two years (4.5.7).
  - **Patients and fairness**: get informed consent and tell patients before AI-assisted diagnosis (4.6.1). Review for bias (4.6.2). Always allow clinician override, with fail-safes (4.6.3).
- **Engineering implication**: Deploy imaging AI on premises or in an approved Omani data centre, and disable vendor telemetry and any inference abroad. Integrate through DICOM, HL7 or FHIR, and plan for MOH integration testing. Treat the AI result as a draft that needs radiologist sign-off. Log overrides. Add a consent or disclosure step to the imaging order workflow. Plan revalidation every two years.
- **Source**: Guidelines for the Use of AI in Radiology, MOH/DGPHE/GUD/018/Vers.01 (Aug 2025), sections cited, URL in Key instruments.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Issued by DGPHE for private radiology departments, but the scope text refers to MOH institutions. It is the only specialty AI guidance found; treat it as indicative for other specialties. Medical-device registration of AI software belongs to the medical devices research.

#### MOH-27: Advertising and patient-facing promotional content [Private]
- **Requirement**: Practitioners may advertise themselves or others only with MOH approval and the fee paid. Advertisements must not mislead, breach public order or morals, or claim qualifications, titles or specialties the person does not hold (MD 231/2024 Art. 42; RD 75/2019 Art. 20). The DGPHE e-service says: get approval before publishing any advertisement in any medium; show the approval number on the advertisement; no superlatives such as "the best", "the newest" or "the first"; offers and discounts need Ministry of Commerce approval; medical-device advertisements need MOH approval; social media promoters sign an undertaking; internal notices inside the facility are exempt if accurate. The fee is OMR 150.
- **Engineering implication**: Patient apps, websites and social posts that promote services or practitioners need a content-approval workflow that stores and displays the DGPHE approval number. Check marketing copy for banned superlatives. Show practitioner titles only from the verified licence register (MOH-21).
- **Source**: MD 231/2024 Art. 42 (Arabic; our translation); RD 75/2019 Art. 20; MOH advertisement permit e-service, https://moh.gov.om/en/services/businesses/private-health-establishments-dgphe/eservice-advertisement-permit/
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Whether in-app service listings and practitioner profiles count as advertising is Unconfirmed.

### G. Telehealth

#### TEL-01: Licensing of telehealth services, centres and platforms [Telehealth] [Private]
- **Requirement**: Practising outside health institutions is banned except in cases set by the Regulation (RD 75/2019 Art. 9). MD 231/2024 Art. 35(6) allows "electronic health services" outside institutions, but only with MOH approval. The facility classification annex of MD 231/2024 includes a "remote healthcare services centre". The National Policy requires the institution to hold an MOH licence to provide telehealth (cl. 8.6.2.2(1)). The Telehealth Guideline (Dec 2025) gives DGPHE these roles for private providers of virtual clinic services: licensing, monitoring and supervision; enforcing MD 109/2020, "which governs licensing and operational standards for private telehealth services"; approving platforms together with MOH's IT directorate (integration and security), the TRA (telecom rules) and MOH legal and licensing; authorising practitioners to practise virtually; keeping registries of approved platforms and licensed virtual-care providers; and handling incidents and complaints (8.3). Platforms must comply with the "approved national telehealth framework" (6.2.1). A 2025 academic paper reports that MOH's 2020 telemedicine licensing requirements set a 2-year licence, renewed 30 days before expiry (secondary).
- **Engineering implication**: Before launch, get the DGPHE facility or service licence and platform approval. Prepare a technical dossier for MOH's IT directorate (security and integration) and confirm the TRA's position. The platform must restrict sessions to MOH-authorised practitioners. Keep registry and approval identifiers in configuration.
- **Source**: RD 75/2019 Art. 9; MD 231/2024 Art. 35(6) and Annex (Arabic; checked against the MOH copy of OG 1561); National Policy cl. 8.6.2.2(1); National Guideline for Telehealth Services 6.2.1, 8.3 and References item 14; Al-Mahrouqi et al., BJPsych International (2025), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-international/article/telepsychiatry-in-oman-within-the-broader-framework-of-telehealth/CD9DEEF1EA61362AEE0A09E11926E307
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for RD 75/2019, MD 231/2024, the policy and the guideline. The content of MD 109/2020 is Unconfirmed (text not located).
- **As of 2026-10**: MD 109/2020 is still cited as current in Dec 2025. MOH plans new virtual services in 2026 (virtual urgent care, remote premarital screening consultations, second opinions).

#### TEL-02: Telehealth platform hosting, security and technical controls [Telehealth]
- **Requirement**: The National Policy's technical controls for telehealth (cl. 8.6.2.1): the purchase contract includes technical support or a service level agreement; telehealth applications are hosted with locally approved telecom companies and data centres; apps run on computers, tablets and phones; users pre-register with a username and password; data exchanged is encrypted; apps are updated regularly; devices have secure, updated protection software; and a protocol covers technical failure or stoppage. The Telehealth Guideline requires platforms to meet the national framework, integrate with the EHR, and have dedicated technical support covering reliability, cybersecurity and user experience (6.2). Teleradiology servers or cloud must be in Oman "in accordance with TRA" (Teleradiology 4.4.4). The TRA regulation on app-based voice and video services covers medical uses (Art. 2). Permit holders must keep in Oman the equipment, servers, control systems, customer call centres and users' personal databases, including billing, chat logs, usage details, payments and any personal information. The system must be hosted in an Omani data centre approved by the TRA (Art. 12(5), 12(6)). Permit holders must also verify subscribers' identity (12(2)), keep user data confidential and retain it for at least 2 years (12(4)), and not offer the service to natural persons (12(1)).
- **Engineering implication**: Host the whole telehealth stack in an approved Omani data centre, including video media and relay servers, chat logs and recordings. Avoid foreign video or messaging services that route media or keep logs abroad unless an approved local deployment exists. Encrypt in transit and document key management. Build in a failure protocol, such as automatic fallback to a phone call. Provide web and mobile clients.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.6.2.1 (Arabic; our translation); Telehealth Guideline 6.2; Teleradiology Guideline 4.4.4; TRA Decision 1152/2/3/2022/1 Arts 2 and 12 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary). Whether a telehealth platform operator itself needs a TRA permit under this regulation is Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: In force.

#### TEL-03: Before the consultation: identity, proxies, introductions, consent and recording [Telehealth]
- **Requirement**: Providers introduce themselves (name, designation, department, institution) and everyone attending or watching, on or off camera, including trainees (Guideline 7.1.4.2, 7.2.1.1). Verify the patient's identity, including affiliated institutions and contacts (7.1.4.3), and ask the patient to state their full name (7.2.1.2). The policy says to verify identity with sufficient evidence, such as name, age, address or phone number (cl. 8.6.2.2(2)). Patients under 18 must have an adult representative, or they are ineligible except in an emergency (7.1.4.4). When someone calls on behalf of another person, record the caller's full name, civil ID number and relationship (7.1.4.5). Confirm the language (7.1.4.7). Informed consent is mandatory before telehealth starts and covers the service scope, data privacy and the patient's rights (7.1.4.8). The policy requires a consent form, preferably electronic, and lets the patient refuse or cancel at any time without giving reasons (cl. 8.6.2.2(20), (21)). Recording a session for research or teaching needs the patient's consent (cl. 8.6.2.2(7)), and any photo or recording falls under cl. 8.7 (MOH-04). Fees are disclosed before the consultation (Guideline 7.1.3, 10.1).
- **Engineering implication**: Build a pre-visit flow with these steps: identity verification; proxy capture (name, civil ID, relationship); a minor check with adult representative; language choice; fee display and acknowledgement; and e-consent with version and timestamp stored in the record. Show the participant list to the patient. Keep recording off by default and allow it only after recorded consent.
- **Source**: Telehealth Guideline 7.1.3, 7.1.4.2 to 7.1.4.8, 7.2.1, 10.1; National Policy cl. 8.6.2.2(2), (3), (7), (8), (20), (21), 8.7 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Current. Whether RD 75/2019 Art. 34 (a third person must be present when a practitioner examines a patient of the other sex) applies to video examinations is Unconfirmed.

#### TEL-04: Documentation of telehealth encounters [Telehealth]
- **Requirement**: Record in the patient's file: the identity of the treating team; the type of telehealth; where the patient was during the session; the date and time; the health problems, assessment, lab tests, prescriptions, referrals and advice given; and every technical incident that affected the session. Keep the entry in sequence with previous visits (cl. 8.6.2.2(4), (5)). The guideline requires documentation in the EHR at the end of the consultation, including that consent was obtained, findings, diagnosis and plan (7.2.7, 7.4.1). Team members may document from outside the institution for remote consultations only on a secure electronic system and under the remote-work controls (cl. 8.5.1(9)). Telehealth data must be integrated with the EHR (6.2.2).
- **Engineering implication**: Add a telehealth encounter type with mandatory fields: team, modality, patient location, start and end time, and a technical incident log captured automatically from the platform. The platform must write back to the EHR rather than keep a separate record.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.5.1(9), 8.6.2.2(4), (5) (Arabic; our translation); Telehealth Guideline 6.2.2, 7.2.7, 7.4.1.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Current.

#### TEL-05: Remote prescribing, sick leave and medication collection [Telehealth] [Prescribing]
- **Requirement**: Prescribing through telehealth is allowed, with caution for controlled substances (Guideline 7.3.6.1). A "prescription referral" can send a prescription to the nearest available facility for patients far away (7.3.6.2). Patients or carers collect medicines from the facility, or use drug delivery where available (7.3.6.3). The onsite pharmacist checks the patient's identity, the prescriber's details and the drug, and has a hotline to the telehealth provider (7.3.6.4). Refills follow sector or institution policy (7.3.6.6). Sick leave can be downloaded from the platform or sent to the patient (7.2.5.5). The law bans prescribing before examining the patient, except in necessity (RD 75/2019 Art. 18).
- **Engineering implication**: Telehealth e-prescriptions must carry the prescriber's identity and licence, and the dispensing pharmacist must be able to verify them (for example by QR code or lookup). Support routing to a chosen facility. Flag controlled drugs for extra checks, or block them, according to institutional policy. Require a documented examination (video or virtual examination fields) before a prescription can be issued.
- **Source**: Telehealth Guideline 7.2.5.5, 7.3.6; RD 75/2019 Art. 18 (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary). Detailed controlled-drug rules for telehealth are Unconfirmed. The CMS guide (Sep 2025) says high-risk and controlled drugs may not be prescribed by telemedicine (secondary, no instrument cited).
- **As of 2026-10**: Current.

#### TEL-06: Clinical safety: triage, exclusions, escalation, failure handling and incidents [Telehealth]
- **Requirement**: Triage before the consultation, using an approved automated system or manually against the institution's inclusion and exclusion criteria (Guideline 7.1.4.1). Send emergencies to emergency services. Do not use telehealth for cases with legal implications, such as abuse, suicide attempts or major trauma; refer them to a facility (7.3.2). Prefer face-to-face care for complex diagnoses, critical results and sensitive concerns (7.3.3). If language or connection problems arise, use an interpreter, reschedule or refer in person (7.3.4). The policy adds: a backup communication plan that the patient knows about (cl. 8.6.2.2(10)); booking a hospital visit where telehealth is unsafe (11); never delaying referral in an emergency (12); a protocol for a medical emergency during a session (23); and a protocol for technical failure (cl. 8.6.2.1(8)). Report critical incidents: actions against medical advice, calls ended early, misuse, medical or technical errors, delayed referrals, unaddressed urgent cases and dissatisfaction that affects outcomes (7.4.3). Track KPIs and patient feedback (9). Telehealth carries the same liability as in-person care (7.3.1).
- **Engineering implication**: Build configurable triage (a questionnaire with red-flag routing to emergency care), exclusion rules, an escalation button for emergency or in-person booking, automatic fallback to phone when video fails, an incident-report form linked to the session, a KPI dashboard and a patient feedback survey.
- **Source**: Telehealth Guideline 7.1.4.1, 7.3.1 to 7.3.4, 7.4.3, 9; National Policy cl. 8.6.2.1(8), 8.6.2.2(10) to (13), (23) (Arabic; our translation).
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary)
- **As of 2026-10**: Current.

#### TEL-07: Practitioners located outside Oman [Telehealth]
- **Requirement**: Medical staff outside Oman who want to provide telehealth to patients in Oman must be registered with MOH. They answer to the courts under the legal system that applies to them (cl. 8.6.2.2(15)). All Omani legal rules for medical staff apply to telehealth staff (cl. 8.6.2.2(14)). Malpractice insurance must cover telehealth (cl. 8.6.2.2(18)). No one may practise without an MOH licence (RD 75/2019 Art. 7). The visiting-practitioner licence lasts up to 3 months and the facility applies for it (MD 231/2024 Arts 23 to 26). DGPHE authorises private practitioners to practise virtually (Guideline 8.3.4). Teleradiologists must be MOH-licensed radiology specialists (Teleradiology 4.2.9). Patient data must not leave Oman without approval (MOH-14).
- **Engineering implication**: Check and store the MOH registration or licence of every remote clinician, including those abroad, and block sessions for clinicians who are not registered. Treat a clinician viewing data from abroad as access from outside Oman: keep hosting in Oman, allow remote viewing only with no local storage, and get an adviser's view on whether CDC approval is needed.
- **Source**: National Policy cl. 8.6.2.2(14), (15), (18) (Arabic; our translation); RD 75/2019 Art. 7; MD 231/2024 Arts 23 to 26; Telehealth Guideline 8.3.4; Teleradiology Guideline 4.2.9.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for these texts. The MOH registration procedure for practitioners abroad is Unconfirmed.
- **As of 2026-10**: No registration procedure or e-service for practitioners abroad was found.

#### TEL-08: Teleradiology services [Telehealth] [Private]
- **Requirement**: The teleradiology guideline is read with MD 109/2020 (section 4.7.4 misprints it as "190/2020"). It sets these rules:
  - **Ownership and staffing**: the facility "should be owned by Omani (as per ministerial Qarar no. 109/2020)" (4.2.1). It needs at least two Omani radiologists licensed by MOH (4.3). Reporting radiologists must be MOH-licensed radiology specialists (4.2.9).
  - **Approval**: radiology must be a registered service, and MOH approves the scope of service before work starts (4.2.10, 4.2.11).
  - **Data and storage**: images move by secure, rapid transfer with patient identifiers on images and reports (4.2.2). Reports are stored under National Records and Archives Authority guidelines, and the referring centre is responsible for retention (4.2.4, 4.2.5). Servers or cloud are in Oman (4.4.4), with a data and disaster recovery policy (4.4.5).
  - **Patients**: tell the patient verbally about the data transfer (4.5.1) and that interpretation is outsourced (4.9.3).
  - **Reporting**: reports follow the MOH medical record policy (4.6). Results go into the base hospital's radiology information system or PACS and the patient record, with the reporting radiologist identified (4.7.1, 4.7.2). Verbal opinions are documented (4.7.6).
  - **Contract**: a contract defines each party's medico-legal responsibilities (4.9.1, 5.2.3).
  - **Equipment (Annex)**: the PACS must be FDA- or CE-approved or approved by MOH's IT directorate, and support the latest DICOM. Diagnostic displays meet minimum specifications, for example 3 MP and DICOM GSDF within 10% for plain film, and 5 MP for mammography.
- **Engineering implication**: Use DICOM-based, encrypted transfer to an Omani-hosted PACS or teleradiology platform. Return reports into the RIS or PACS and the EHR with the reporting radiologist's identity. Record that the patient was informed. Configure display quality assurance. Keep a disaster recovery plan. Commercially, check the Omani-ownership rule before offering foreign-owned teleradiology.
- **Source**: Guideline of Teleradiology Service, MOH/DGPHE/GUD/011/Vers.01 (Jan 2025), sections cited, URL in Key instruments.
- **Confidence**: Confirmed (primary) for the guideline. The underlying MD 109/2020 ownership rule is Unconfirmed (text not seen).
- **As of 2026-10**: Current. Some definitions in the guideline seem copied from another jurisdiction ("Clinical Operating Permit", "Registry of Companies", "Healthcare Operators Regulation"); do not rely on them.

- **AI**: MOH plans AI decision support and predictive analytics (2026 to 2027). The AI-in-radiology guideline is due for review in Aug 2028 and requires revalidation of AI systems every two years.

## Questions for advisers

- **MD 109/2020 (telemedicine licensing)**: what are its full text, issue date and Gazette status? Does the Omani-ownership condition apply to all telehealth providers or only teleradiology? What are the platform approval criteria? Does a telehealth platform operator also need a TRA permit under Decision 1152/2/3/2022/1?
- **Approved health information systems**: what criteria and process does DGPHE use to approve a private facility's electronic health information system (MD 231/2024 Arts 8(4) and 29)? Is there a list of approved systems? Is connection to the national health record a condition?
- **Legal force of the National Policy (May 2024)**: is it binding as such? It was not found in the Gazette. The DGPHE guideline (2025) calls it "the core legislation" and says breaches lead to penalties under the medical practice law. How does it sit alongside the PDPL? What does cl. 9.1 mean when it says data is "property of the State" and needs "written … consent"?
- **Retention periods**: what are the actual NRAA retention periods for health records? Is there a final destruction point after the "intermediate" stage? The DGPHE retention schedule (Annex III of P003-2012) is not in the published PDF.
- **Internal conflicts in MOH documents** (advisers should say which wins):
  - Patient breach notice: 10 working days from the breach (cl. 9.13) or from the end of the investigation (cl. 23.7(3))?
  - Sick-leave diagnosis: shown (DGPHE 3.5) or hidden without consent (cl. 8.5.1(5))?
  - Verbal-order authentication: 24 hours (DGPHE 2.1.13) or 48 hours (2.6)?
- **Electronic consent**: does MOH accept e-signed consent on its own forms (RD 75/2019 Art. 37 "form prepared by the Ministry")? Which signature level under RD 39/2025 (simple, advanced or certified) does MOH expect for consent and prescriptions?
- **Research**: has the ministerial decision on conditions for human research (RD 75/2019 Art. 28) been issued? What is the legal basis of the MOH research committees? What rules apply to research by private institutions, and to exporting research data?
- **E-prescription**: is there a national e-prescription mandate or exchange for the private sector? None was found apart from the Dhamani insurance platform.
- **Standards**: no MOH mandate was found for SNOMED CT, LOINC or ICD-11. ICD-10 is required for private-sector statistics. FHIR is used for the national record, but no public implementation guide was found. DICOM and HL7/FHIR appear only in the radiology AI and teleradiology guidelines.
- **AI**: does RD 75/2019 Art. 17 ("unlicensed means or methods") require MOH approval of clinical AI tools beyond the radiology guideline? Does the radiology AI guideline bind MOH facilities?
- **Practitioners abroad**: what is the procedure for the MOH registration required by cl. 8.6.2.2(15)? Does remote viewing from abroad count as "processing outside Oman" under cl. 13.7?
- **Notifications**: is RD 73/92 still fully in force alongside RD 43/2025? Which electronic channel (Tarassud, Al-Shifa) must private facilities use? Statutory deadlines for birth and death registration were not researched.
- **Video examinations**: does RD 75/2019 Art. 34 (third person present for an examination of a patient of the other sex) apply to video consultations?
